# 09 · Titles, annotations, and explanatory language

**Source:** Dykes ch. 6, PDF pp. 200–211 (Aha and trailer); ch. 8, pp. 269–280, figs. 8.12–8.15; Knaflic chs. 4, 5, and 7. Writing models are adaptations.

## Title according to the task

Dykes distinguishes descriptive and explanatory titles (p. 276). When telling a story, use the title to state the message the visualization supports; for lookup, exploration, or recurring monitoring, a descriptive title may be more appropriate. Do not demand an artificial insight.

| Function | Adaptable template | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| Description | Metric + dimension/period | Net sales by month |
| Insight | Metric + change + comparison | Net sales fell Q2 thousand compared with May |
| Contrast | A exceeds B by a relevant magnitude | North contributes more revenue than South, but lower margin |
| Implication | Insight + demonstrated or conditional consequence | The budget gap widened to Q20 thousand |
| Decision | Result + reasonable action | Prioritize diagnosing returns before increasing investment |

Rules: clear verb and subject; one dominant assertion; unit and reference when they change interpretation; numbers rounded consistently with chart; avoid unsupported superlatives and implied causality. Do not repeat the same number in title, label, legend, and paragraph unless this reinforces understanding. Do not declare a future effect as realized. Use the magnitude that best represents the data, not the most impactful one.

## Subtitles, labels, and notes

Separate message from definitions: title interprets; subtitle identifies metric, period, unit, scope, and denominator when needed. Label provides value or identifies series; source enables verification. Notes explain limitations, methodology, or assumptions that do not fit next to the data. If a condition materially changes the message, it must be near the insight, not buried in fine print.

## Annotations (Dykes pp. 276–278)

- Observational: points out a visible feature (maximum, intersection, gap). Example: “Q12 thousand in June.”
- Additive: provides verified context external to the chart. Example: “Customer definition changed in July,” if documented by the source.
- Hybrid: combines observation and context. Example: “Median time fell 3 min after the system changed,” only if the change is known; “after” does not prove “because of.”

Link the note to the relevant point, interval, or group through proximity or a subtle line; do not obscure data. Use few, concise notes in natural order. A standalone report may need more explanation. A causal note requires causal evidence; if a hypothesis, state it as such.

## Microcopy procedure (adaptation)

1. Write the verifiable assertion and its reference.
2. Reduce to one idea without losing meaning or uncertainty.
3. Select words that match the visual code.
4. Move unit and period to subtitle if it improves reading.
5. Add annotations only to guide an important comparison or provide context.
6. Review titles in sequence; avoid contradictions and topic jumps. Verify that the chart demonstrates what the title claims.

Correction examples: “The program was a success” → “Reported satisfaction rose from 30% to 55%; the causal effect is unknown.” “The North region is underperforming” → “North was 8 points below its conversion target” (if target is documented). “Causes of churn” → “Factors associated with churn in the sample,” if only correlation is known. Do not include examples as the user's results.
