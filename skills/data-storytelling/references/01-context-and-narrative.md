# 01 · Context and narrative construction

Based on Knaflic chapters 1 (pp. 30–46) and 7 (pp. 180–203) of the transcription. Worksheets and examples are adaptations.

## Diagnosis before designing

| Dimension | Questions | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | Who has authority? What do they care about, know, and object to? | Primary audience and possible secondary versions |
| Presenter | Is there trust? Must methodology be demonstrated? | Conclusion or evidence first |
| Objective | What should they know, remember, decide, or do? | Expected outcome |
| Evidence | What data supports and contradicts the message? What is missing? | Robust findings and limitations |
| Medium | Live, standalone reading, hybrid, interactive dashboard? | Density, sequence, and notes |
| Tone | Celebration, alert, learning, neutral evaluation? | Intensity and style |
| Constraints | Time, brand, accessibility, space, format? | Scope and design |

If executives need a decision and analysts need methodology, deliver a summary with an appendix or two versions. For recurring reports, clarify what changes each period and which metrics must remain stable. Avoid overly broad audiences.

## Exploratory versus explanatory

Exploratory: open questions, multiple views, tested hypotheses, explicit uncertainty. Explanatory: select relevant discoveries, explain meaning, and provide adequate support. Do not transfer the entire exploratory effort to the audience or suppress contradictory results. Transition when a specific, bounded claim can be defended.

## Big Idea, three-minute story, and storyboard

The Big Idea (a concept attributed to Nancy Duarte in the book) expresses a point of view, what is at stake, and a complete sentence. It is not a nominal topic. Template: “[Verifiable finding] implies [consequence for audience]; therefore [decision or next step if appropriate].” Do not force action or causality.

Three-minute story: tell without slides why the question arises, what you observed, why it matters, and what you propose; adapt to 30 seconds and an extended version if required. Slides are support, not text to read aloud.

Storyboard: work first with cards or sketches, one per message; identify evidence and link to the next; group, move, remove, and add until the sequence is coherent; seek validation of the approach when possible; produce afterward. Worksheet: number | takeaway title | question | supporting data | chart | transition | notes/appendix.

## Three acts

Beginning: situation, essential background, problem, objective, and known consequences of inaction. Middle: evidence, comparisons, alternatives, or tension between current and desired states. End: conclusion, implication, recommendation, or alternatives; connect to the beginning. Avoid fictional conflict; a target gap or a decision with real costs may suffice. If no grounded recommendation exists, conclude with open questions.

## Order and checks

Chronological/analytical order for credibility when appropriate; conclusion first when time is short, trust is established, or results are the priority. Do not present every analysis. Strategic repetition (“bing, bang, bongo”): preview, develop, and recap. Horizontal logic: read titles in sequence; vertical logic: content supports the title; reverse storyboard: one sentence per page and verify flow. Ask an unfamiliar reader what they understood, remember, and would do without giving away the answer.

## Medium

Oral: presenter controls pace, few ideas per view, reveal and separate notes. Standalone: reader controls path, visible context, explanation, and sources. Hybrid (slideument): clear main body and detail in notes/appendix; do not depend on animation lost in print.
