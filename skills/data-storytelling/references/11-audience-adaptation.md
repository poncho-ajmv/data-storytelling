# 11 · Audience, psychology, and communication of change

**Source:** Dykes chs. 1–4, PDF pp. 23–143, especially 50–58, 69–104, 130–143; Knaflic chs. 1 and 7. Neuroscience claims in the book are not presented as universal laws; responsible communication practices are derived from them.

## Three components and communication goals

Dykes (figs. 2.1–2.2, pp. 50–52) combines data, narrative, and visuals. Data + narrative explain; data + visuals enlighten by revealing patterns; narrative + visuals engage the audience. All three complement each other to guide understanding and possible change. The goal is not merely to deliver information. Dykes proposes four levels (pp. 56–57): capture attention, achieve understanding, facilitate recall, and encourage action. Evaluate in that order without assuming that a story guarantees persuasion.

Chapter 2 includes five rhetorical perspectives: ethos (credibility), logos (reasoning), pathos (emotional connection), telos (purpose), and kairos (timing). In the skill: traceable evidence; verifiable reasoning; real human consequences; explicit purpose; appropriate timing. Do not use emotion to replace facts or pressure decisions, or present correlation as proof.

## Eight audience factors (Dykes ch. 4, pp. 135–138)

| Factor | How to adapt |
|---|---|
| Goals and priorities | Connect to a relevant objective, not what the analyst finds striking |
| Beliefs and preferences | Acknowledge their perspective, show contrary evidence respectfully |
| Specific expectations | Answer the expected question; declare if answering another |
| Timing | Consider real horizon and urgency, not manufactured urgency |
| Topic familiarity | Adjust vocabulary and background |
| Data literacy | Explain unfamiliar metrics, axes, and codes without infantilizing |
| Level of responsibility | Adjust detail, autonomy, and available time |
| Audience mix | Design for primary recipient and leave layers/appendix for others |

Worksheet: who decides? Who executes? Who has expertise? What do they expect? What reasonable objection do they have? What information do they need to evaluate? What constraints exist? Do not stereotype by role or assume knowledge without indications. Adaptation changes selection, language, and density, never essential data or its limitations.

## Relationship to insight and delivery format

Dykes (table 1.1, p. 32): personal audience, organizational insider, or outsider. An insider may have context and trust but also a conflict of interest; an outsider can bring perspective and need more context or support. If controversial, prior meetings with key people to identify gaps, objections, alternatives, and evidence needs (p. 139); not to silence dissent. Maintain one version of the facts.

Direct communication (presentation, dialogue): enables clarification, graduated layers, and guided reading. Indirect (email, report, infographic): more contextual text and anchors; each separately shareable visual must preserve its source and meaning. Time-constrained executives: Data Trailer and access to detail. Technical users: accessible method and checks; do not overwhelm others with the full analysis journey.

## Psychology and resistance (ch. 3)

Audiences do not process facts in a vacuum. Prior knowledge, interests, emotions, trust, and biases affect interpretation and acceptance. Do not expect a correct table to persuade by itself. If results contradict a prior explanation, present a supported alternative explaining the pattern, limitations, and evidence that would be needed; do not merely deny the previous explanation. Seek an unfamiliar reader's feedback to identify the curse of knowledge. Do not use decontextualized neuroscience claims, popular percentages about emotional decisions, or manipulative techniques.

## Role-based adaptation (fictional example, not Dykes's categories)

Net sales fall because of an accounting increase in returns: leadership needs magnitude and impact; sales, segments and verifiable reasons; finance, gross–returns–net reconciliation; operations, process and verified cases; analysts, definitions, supporting data, and hypotheses. The same central evidence must be consistent; each receives what their task requires. If the contributing segment is unknown, do not invent it.
