# 08 · Narrative architectures, arc, and storyboard

**Source:** Dykes, ch. 4, PDF pp. 110–118; ch. 6, pp. 185–223, particularly 191–211 and figs. 6.4–6.13. Knaflic, chs. 1 and 7. Translations in the Spanish version were our own; templates and format decisions are adaptations.

## Anatomy: six elements of a data story

1. **Data Foundation:** a nonfiction story supported by solid data.
2. **Main Point:** a central insight that unifies the whole.
3. **Explanatory Focus:** explain meaning, not merely list what happened; distinguish what is known about how and why from what remains uncertain.
4. **Linear Sequence:** ordered points that build understanding; each builds on the previous one. Not necessarily chronological order.
5. **Dramatic Elements:** context, real tension, people, or relevant consequences. Do not dramatize through fiction.
6. **Visual Anchors:** visualizations or other elements that make evidence visible and enable comparisons.

Apply flexibly: a one-number story can fit in a paragraph and a chart. Do not require every report to have six sections.

## Data Storytelling Arc

Dykes adapts Freytag's arc in four stages (fig. 6.4, pp. 191–193):

| Original stage | Function | Design question |
|---|---|---|
| Setting, with Hook | Minimal context and noteworthy observation that introduces an opportunity or problem | What does the audience expect, and what catches attention? |
| Rising Insights | Develop relevant evidence layer by layer | What do they need to know to understand the central point? |
| Aha Moment | Communicate the main insight and its meaning | What changes in understanding and why does it matter? |
| Solution and Next Steps | Examine alternatives and guide the decision | What action, test, or conversation is reasonable? |

The Hook may be an observation; the Aha is a meaningful insight. Do not confuse them or manufacture suspense. If the audience demands an immediate conclusion, use a Data Trailer; do not hide relevant information for effect. Knaflic's Big Idea (point of view and what is at stake) is compatible with the Aha: define it before designing, even if it is revealed after context during a presentation.

## Nine types of story points

Dykes, pp. 196–198, fig. 6.7; the author draws inspiration from Ben Jones. Select only those needed:

| Type | Question | Suggested visual |
|---|---|---|
| Change over time | What changed and when? | Lines, columns, slopegraphs |
| Relationship | How do two variables vary together? | Scatterplot; does not imply causation |
| Intersection | When does one series exceed or fall below another? | Lines with common scale |
| Project forward | What is expected under the assumptions? | Distinct forecast line and uncertainty |
| Compare and contrast | What is similar or different? | Bars, dots, pairs, table |
| Drill down | What components explain the aggregate? | Bars, small multiples, waterfall |
| Zoom out | How does an observation fit within a larger universe? | Longer series, panels, reference |
| Cluster | What concentrations appear? | Scatterplot, histogram, map if appropriate |
| Outlier | Which point departs from the rest? | Scatterplot, series, distribution, point label |

A story point is not always a chart; a simple comparison can be expressed in text. Select the type by evidence, not to fill a catalog.

## Storyboard: design backward, communicate forward

Dykes, pp. 199–209, figs. 6.9–6.12. First identify the Aha Moment; then setting and hook; then intermediate points; finally solutions and next steps. Building this way avoids paths that do not lead to the central insight. When presenting, choose the sequence appropriate for the audience.

Scene worksheet (adaptation): ID | function in arc | explanatory title | question it answers | data and reference | visual | transition | objection/limitation | appendix. Rehearse the storyboard without software before investing time in design. Remove tangential points, retain material counterevidence, and order to anticipate relevant objections. Apply Knaflic's horizontal logic (read titles in sequence) and vertical logic (each page demonstrates its title).

## Data Trailer

Dykes, pp. 209–211. For an impatient audience, brief setting + hook + early revelation of the Aha. Allows them to know what they gain by continuing to listen. If time permits, continue with rising insights and next steps; otherwise leave support and a concrete request. Do not use the trailer as an excuse to avoid showing evidence. In a written executive summary, keep conclusion and minimum support together.

## Application patterns (adaptation, not Dykes's original classification)

- Situation → problem → supported explanation → solution or test: operational diagnosis.
- Current state → real target → gap → response: performance.
- Before → after → difference and limitations → next step: evaluation.
- Central result → evidence → implication → decision: executive summary.
- Expectation → reality → contrast → response: anomalies or hypotheses.
- Trend → inflection → context → consequence: evolution.
- Overview → detail → synthesis: drill down.
- Alternatives → comparable criteria → justified recommendation: choice.
- Hypothesis → evidence for/against → bounded conclusion: research.

These variations apply the arc or trailer, not unrelated methodologies. They are not Dykes's nine story points: the latter describe evidence patterns, not full structures.

## People, conflict, and analogies

Dykes, pp. 212–222. If data represents people, identify their role; construct a data-based persona, give it identity and voice only with verifiable testimony, and, if evidence exists, show its journey. A synthetic character must be marked as illustrative; do not present an invented quote as real. Conflict = distance between current and desired state, unexpected results, or incompatible alternatives. Use prediction questions, information gaps, or comparisons to engage the audience only if transparency is preserved. Analogies: verify familiarity, validity, clarity, concision, and interest; do not equate phenomena that do not share the relevant relationship.

If analysis cannot support a solution recommendation, the ending is a well-defined problem and an invitation for the appropriate audience to agree on the next step (Dykes p. 209).
