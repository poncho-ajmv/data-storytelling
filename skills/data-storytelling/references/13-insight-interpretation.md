# 13 · Interpretation, actionability, and analytical discipline

**Source:** Dykes ch. 1, PDF pp. 29–37; ch. 5, pp. 146–183, especially 157–176; ch. 6, pp. 199–211; Knaflic chs. 1, 7, and 8. Worksheets are working adaptations.

## The “So what?” test

Dykes draws on Avinash Kaushik's approach (p. 158) and develops it into six attributes. Ask: why does this audience care? What can they do about it? What is its potential impact? If the answer is unclear, investigate or communicate as information, not as an insight ready for action.

| Question | Attribute (Dykes, pp. 159–160) | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Why does it matter? | Valuable | Potential benefit or risk is meaningful for this audience |
| Why does it matter? | Relevant | Fits audience priorities and responsibilities |
| What to do? | Practical | There is a feasible course of action or reasonable investigation |
| What to do? | Specific | Evidence identifies where, when, how much, or how, without inferring unproven causes |
| What impact? | Concrete | Understandable magnitude, quantified if possible, with clear assumptions |
| What impact? | Contextualized | Sufficient comparison, reference, or context to assess significance |

Do not turn these six attributes into a truth index or require a score for every chart. A valid insight with no immediate action can still be useful. An attractive potential impact does not compensate for defective evidence. Specificity does not authorize inventing a causal explanation.

## Distinguish levels of assertion

Adaptation of the value chain (fig. 1.2, p. 33) and explanatory focus (ch. 4):

| Level | Hypothetical example | Validation |
|---|---|---|
| Data | Q900 thousand in June | Source, period, and unit |
| Information / observation | −10% vs. May | Prior value Q1 million, calculation, and comparability |
| Insight | Net sales fell while gross sales grew; returns explain the accounting difference | Reconciled identity and confirmed breakdown |
| Implication | The gap against the quarterly target widened, if a documented target exists | Real target, horizon, and arithmetic |
| Action | Investigate returns and test causes by category | Owner and resources known or pending |

Use explicit labels: observed, calculated, inferred, hypothesis, forecast, and proposed. Impact estimation requires formula, base, horizon, and scenario. Annualize only with defensible assumptions (e.g., stable monthly spending × 12 months) and mark as an estimate, not guaranteed savings. If costs are missing, do not calculate ROI. If a target was not provided, do not silently assume it. Avoid proposing an impractical solution simply to complete the arc (p. 209).

## Discipline against biases and fallacies (ch. 5, pp. 168–176)

- Confirmation bias: actively seek evidence contradicting the hypothesis; review cases where the pattern fails.
- Survivorship bias: check who is absent from the dataset. Active customer rates do not represent those who churned.
- Curse of knowledge: analyst familiarity makes explanation to beginners difficult; seek an outsider's reading and define metrics.
- Correlation: do not confuse coincidence or association with causation. Investigate confounders, temporal order, and alternatives.
- Texas sharpshooter: if many relationships were explored and a significant one selected, identify the exploration and seek independent confirmation; do not claim significance without a relevant test.
- Hasty generalization: acknowledge sample size, selection, and representativeness, without improperly extrapolating to unobserved populations.

## Evidence log (adaptation)

For each relevant conclusion document: assertion; source ID; data and calculation; universe and period; evidence type; alternative explanations; material limitation; qualitative confidence (grounded, not an invented percentage); next check. If the conclusion changes during design, revise the title, arc, and visual rather than seeking data that fits the old title.

### Conditions for proceeding

- If the central insight is supported and its scope known, build the narrative and show limitations.
- If it is an observation without explanation, communicate it as such or investigate; propose questions, not causes.
- If evidence of impact magnitude is missing, describe its direction or relevance conditionally.
- If two interpretations are plausible, show alternatives and the evidence that would distinguish them.
- If a decision depends on a sensitive assumption, make it visible before recommending.
