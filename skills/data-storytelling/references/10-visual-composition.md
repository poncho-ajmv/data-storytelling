# 10 · Visual composition of scenes and complete artifacts

**Source:** Dykes, ch. 7, pp. 228–257; ch. 8, pp. 260–302, especially tables 8.1–8.7 and figs. 8.12–8.15; Knaflic chs. 3–5 and 7–9. The following page layouts are application adaptations, not official templates.

## Seven principles of visual storytelling

Dykes divides them into setting scenes (ch. 7) and refining them (ch. 8):

1. Visualize the right data: adjust, add, or remove relevant context without distortion.
2. Choose the right visualizations: function, perceptual encoding, and audience.
3. Calibrate the visuals to the message: nearby comparisons, shared baseline, and consistency.
4. Remove unnecessary noise: excess data, overlap, and purposeless ornamentation.
5. Focus attention: color, titles, annotations, typography, and progressive reveal.
6. Make data approachable and engaging: readability, reference lines, format, conventions, relevance, and understandable scale.
7. Instill trust in the numbers: accuracy, sources, scales, and review.

They complement Knaflic's six lessons; they do not replace them. Decide on data and comparison first, aesthetics second. Do not confuse a scene with a slide: a scene can be revealed in layers or occupy part of a page.

## Scene anatomy (proposal)

- Header: takeaway title when supported; minimal context beneath.
- Evidence area: dominant visual with sufficient alignment and space. If supporting visuals exist, they should be comparable and subordinate to the main one.
- Focus: one primary cue; data-anchored annotations, selected values, subtle references.
- Scene ending: implication or transition if it contributes to understanding. Do not repeat the entire title.
- Footer: source, unit, assumptions, coverage, or relevant limitations; leave secondary data and method in appendix when medium allows.

Distribute space by importance, not by a fixed template. A KPI card may have no chart; a standalone document needs context alongside evidence. Respect consistent margins, common alignment, horizontal text, and white space. In Spanish (as in English), reading generally runs left to right and top to bottom; modify it with contrast or numbering if the sequence of understanding requires it. Verify at actual size.

## Multiple visuals together

Use several charts only if they answer complementary questions. If comparing the same variables or periods, align scales, axes, order, and color meanings. If scales differ for a justified reason, clearly mark it. If charts are standalone, each needs sufficient context. Reduce repetitive visuals to a comparison view when this facilitates the task. A matrix of small multiples is preferable to a spaghetti chart when series overlap, but each panel must retain legibility and consistent scale.

## Layering and delivery

Progressive reveal of a complex chart (Dykes fig. 8.15, pp. 278–280): introduce axes and unit meanings; introduce main series; show reference and focal data point; add context and conclusion. Do not use ornamental animation or temporarily hide evidence that changes the conclusion. In static material, use a complete view with annotations or a series of consistent views; check print and distribution versions.

## Supported refinement tactics

- Reduce secondary data: remove if it does not change interpretation; group minor categories under “Other” when this does not hide material variation; separate overlap into panels. Communicate grouping criteria and keep detail available (pp. 262–266).
- Reference lines: known targets, appropriate average, defined bands, anchor lines in scatterplots, or subtle gridlines for comparison. Do not add arbitrary thresholds or unsupported fit lines (table 8.3, pp. 284–286).
- Formatting: horizontal and direct labels; natural intervals; logical order; width/height ratio that does not distort perception; transparency for overlapping points; jitter only if artificial positioning is explained and the value of interest is not altered. If smoothing a series, identify the method and do not hide relevant fluctuations (tables 8.2 and 8.4, pp. 283, 287–288).
- Conventions: semantic consistency of color and direction, time on X-axis, easily interpreted comparisons. Do not use red/green as sole cue. When colors have a known association, leverage it without compromising legibility (table 8.5, p. 290).
- Images and icons: only if they anchor meaning or make magnitude relatable to the audience; never as decoration or evidence of an undocumented event. A visual analogy requires proportional scale and verifiable calculation (pp. 291–295).

## Review visual trust

Table 8.6, pp. 297–299: bars and areas start at zero; lines need not start at zero if the scale is declared and does not exaggerate. Proportional date intervals, do not choose periods to hide context, justified binning intervals, bubbles proportional by area (not radius), identified sources. Review spelling, sums, values, and labels. The author's aspect-ratio recommendation is indicative: do not impose a ratio if it harms context or task.
